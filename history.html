The poineer of photography

Joseph Nicéphore Niépce

In 1826, Joseph Nicéphore Niépce captured the first permanent photograph, titled View from the Window at Gras, using a camera obscura and a bitumen-coated pewter plate. The image required an exposure time of over eight hours, resulting in a blurry picture due to changing light conditions. Niépce’s experiments with silver chloride and halide were limited by image darkening over time. Later, he partnered with Louis Jacques Mandé Daguerre, who advanced the process using silver-plated copper and mercury vapor, and by 1839, hypo was introduced to stabilize photographs against light sensitivity.

Camera obscura

The camera obscura, meaning "dark room" in Latin, is an optical device that projects an inverted image of an external scene through a small aperture. First noted by the Han philosopher Mozi around 470-391 BC, it demonstrated that light travels in straight lines. By the mid-16th century, lenses were added, enhancing its use for artists, while portable versions appeared in the late 17th century, allowing for more accurate drawing. Although early images had to be traced by hand, the camera obscura set the stage for photography. Johann Zahn envisioned a compact camera in 1685, but it wasn't until the 19th century, with innovators like Joseph Nicéphore Niépce, that practical photographic cameras emerged.

First photo

In 1826, using this process, Niépce took the earliest surviving 'photograph'—a view from a window of his house in Chalons-sur-Saône which required an exposure of about 8 hours! This image is now preserved as part of the Gernsheim Collection at the University of Texas.
